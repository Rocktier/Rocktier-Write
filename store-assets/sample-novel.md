# The Library of Low Tide

*Opening chapters of a novel — written in Rocktier Write*

## Chapter One — Ebb

The library opens only at low tide.

The keeper says the shelves extend as far as the water withdraws, and the rows that drown at high tide reopen with the next full moon. No one remembers who built the place. It opens for six hours a day, never more than a minute early or late, keeping time like something older than people.

On the third row after the ebb, Chen Wanzhou found the book. Its spine carried no title, yet it felt warm, the way paper feels when someone has only just closed it — faintly salted. Tucked inside was a borrower's card, and on the card was her mother's name, dated tomorrow.

## Chapter Two — The Keeper's Ledger

The lighthouse log lived in the same ledger, one month to a page, unbroken for thirty years.

The last entry was hurried: *The tide is wrong. It has gone out too far — far enough to see the door at the bottom of the sea.* Below it, half a page of nothing. Then a new line in a different hand, calm to the point of courtesy: *The door is open. You are welcome.*

She photographed the page. The shutter clicked, and every lamp in the room went out at once, leaving only the screen glowing — a small stretch of beach after the ebb.

## Chapter Three — A Catalogue of the Unnamed

The catalogue drawers were labelled not with titles but with names.

She pulled the drawer marked **Chen**. The cards ran in order by year, the oldest from 1974. The newest card was still crisp at the edges. It bore her own name, and beneath it a single line: *not yet returned.*

The borrower's card warmed in her pocket. All at once she understood the keeper's broken sentence: nothing here was ever lent out to be read.

## Chapter Four — Before the Flood

Forty minutes until high tide. At last, the keeper spoke.

"Your mother borrowed a book thirty years ago," he said. "The day she returned it, the sea took her name out of every register and every family tree. Every page you find here is a page she never finished writing."

A bell rang. Far out, a white line was advancing across the horizon — unhurried, and entirely without doubt.

She closed the book and noticed, for the first time, the sentence printed on the back of the flyleaf, one she had read a dozen times without ever seeing:

**Write what matters where the tide cannot take it.**

She took out her pen, and on the back of the borrower's card, letter by letter, she wrote down her own name.
